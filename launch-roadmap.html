3.3 unit economics — rebuilt to the 70% floor (2026-08-23)

The floor is a formula, not a number: retail floor = landed COGS ÷ 0.30. Never price below 70% gross margin on landed COGS. 75–80% is the sweet spot; the placeholders below sit deliberately near the low end of it, because early on we would rather be competitive and build loyalty than protect a point of margin. Two constraints bound it from the other side: the hero and the moisturizer stay above $30, and at 80% DTC margin a 50% wholesale discount still leaves a healthy 60% B2B margin — at 70% it leaves 40%, and retail distribution stops working. So the floor is a floor, not a target.

3.4 break-even and the elephant

The elephant, named — and deliberately accepted on Aug 23. Filling 10,000 units against a realistic launch window of 100–500 orders and a realistic first year of $50–100K would mean most filled inventory sits 18+ months. Both partners looked at this directly and held Option A anyway, on one condition that changes the shape of the risk: we do not fill 10,000 units. We buy 10,000 components per SKU because the 10K tier costs less in total than the 5K tier, and we fill in batches sized to actual demand. Components don't expire; the fill does.

At the midpoint (~$165K deployed, ~$48 average contribution per order) break-even is roughly 2,900–3,400 orders ≈ $200–235K revenue — a mid/late-2027 event, not a 2026 one. Note: the revised pricing in §3.3 lowers average contribution, so the order count at break-even rises — recompute this line once Pravada's real unit costs land.

The protective moves, in order of power: ① size each fill batch to real demand, never to the component count; ② confirm shelf life per SKU in writing (answered: 2 years unopened, PAO ~12 months); ③ ring-fence a cash floor for marketing and operations — a warehouse of product with no audience budget is how launches die quietly; ④ re-forecast fill #2 from real sell-through at day 60, not from hope.

9 · marketing.

One system, three layers: receipts (trust), statement drops (acquisition), the routine (economics). Thesis: earn trust with receipts, launch in statements, sell the routine.

9.1 receipts — the credibility engine

  • The launch's best stories already happened in the formulation work: we removed witch hazel and lavender from our own hero before selling it; we swapped the clogging emollient for squalane. The edits are the receipts — no competitor can tell this story about our formulas.
  • Formats: "read the label with us" founder videos · the refuse list (one refused ingredient per post) · ingredient SEO pages (multi-molecular hyaluronic acid, oat milk cleanser sensitive skin, non-comedogenic peptide moisturizer, essence vs toner) · the parked "named." template as the recurring ingredient-hero format.
  • Rules: every number sourced, source directly beneath the claim; smaller true numbers beat bigger fake ones; knowledgeable friend, never the callout account; never name competitors.

9.2 statement drops — the launch architecture

  • Sell numbered statements, not a catalog: "statement 01: hydration." Labels carry statement 01 — hydration. as a system line; the routine poem rides a business-card-grade insert.

The engine is micro and nano creators on an affiliate model — they earn commission on sales attributed to their content, so they cost nothing until they sell.   • Scope the unboxing experience and a sensory "wow" moment as part of the creator strategy, not after it. The category rewards a thing that is fun to film. Ask Pravada and UA what is possible inside the existing formulas and components.

  • Hero offer: the 4-SKU routine bundle ("the full statement.", $112 placeholder vs $132 separates — revised to the 70% floor, §3.3). Serum is the single-SKU entry door; flows upgrade serum buyers to the routine.

11 · realistic expectations.

  • Waitlist math: waitlists convert at under 5%. 2,000–5,000 emails → 100–500 launch-window orders TOTAL (not per SKU). That's a good launch for a bootstrapped brand with no prior audience.
  • Launch-window revenue: 100–500 orders at ~$60–90 AOV ≈ $7–40K. The inventory does not sell out in January — it isn't supposed to.
  • Year one: Beauty-Independent benchmarks for a solo bootstrapped founder: $50K is a good year, $100K is fantastic. That expectation does NOT change because the inventory got bigger — the inventory is a multi-year position (§3.4), and the plan must survive on year-one revenue without touching the cash floor.
  • What compounds: repeat rate on the 60-day cycle, subscription take-rate, review velocity, and email list growth. Those four numbers are the business (and the future Sephora pitch). Vanity spikes are not.
  • Success checkpoints: day 30 — 150+ orders cumulative, 3.5%+ site CVR from the warm list, first organic UGC. Day 90 — 400+ orders, 15%+ repeat/subscription attach on early cohorts, one content format reliably producing. Day 365 — $50–100K revenue, 2,500+ list, fill #2 sized from data. If day-90 numbers are far under, the fix is audience and offer — not more inventory.
  • Smaller and sold-through beats big and parked. Confirmed 2026-08-23: SKU count and fill size were never what an eventual retail pitch needed. Repeat rate, subscription take-rate and review velocity are — and those are indifferent to how many units sit in the office.
  • The pattern to guard against (named with love): exploration-as-procrastination. Five site directions, ~40 packaging renders, and a 14-day-unsent email preceded the July roadmap — and then seven weeks passed in which none of the ten gates moved. That is the evidence base for the honest bandwidth number in §13. The countermeasures now in place: a weekly 30-minute sync, a daily gate count posted to Teams, and a pre-committed fallback on every gate so "no decision" defaults to the fallback rather than to drift.
